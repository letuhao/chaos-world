# Longmarrow

Four centuries, and a body made out of the one material it may never stop replacing.

**Why they exist.** Longmarrow are the setting's answer to a question nobody else was
asked: what does a species look like if it is optimised for *keeping* rather than for
getting? Their qi lives in bone marrow that turns over at roughly a percent a year,
which is why they live four hundred years and why every gate on the ladder has been
passed by one of them at some point. There is no ceiling on this body.

**What the success costs.** Turnover is slow, and therefore everything they do is
slow. Breakthroughs are certain and late. A Longmarrow holds a body for centuries and
changes nothing about it, and the result is that **nothing they have ever built
survived to be inherited intact** — which is the actual limitation on this species,
and it is not anatomical at all.

**The closed path.** The body path, and the reason is the whole species in one line:
hardening bone marrow stops the turnover the qi depends on. A body-cultivated
Longmarrow suffocates on its own reserves inside a decade. The longest-lived body in
the record is built out of the one material it is forbidden to make permanent.

**Reproduction, and the demographic consequence.** Four hundred days of gestation and
fertility near a sixth of a Commonborn's, across a four-century life, means the
population is effectively static. Every Longmarrow is individually irreplaceable.
This is the single fact that has organised everything about them socially: they
cannot afford to lose anybody, and they cannot afford to start a project they will
not finish.

**Culture.** This is the one species here where lifespan *does* imply something, and
it is worth being precise about why: four hundred years of unbroken context makes
knowledge transmission a biological necessity rather than a social preference. The
result is not a culture so much as an obligation — a record that has to be kept,
because the alternative is four hundred years of someone else's memory.
